> <center><i>Discussing and Refining Our Ideas</i></center>

The most challenging part of the project was coming up with an intellectual order for the Chinese Scholarship Committee Records. An intellectual order, typically presented in the form of a processing plan, details how an archivist plans on arranging the collection: What series would there be? What would physical processing look like? What preservational issues might they face? How would researchers interact with the new arrangement? Intellectual processing means laying all of these concerns out, talking them through, and constructing a general outline of how the collection should be. 

To ease into this process, we first discussed archival standards and processing procedures with Allison and Bethany, getting to know the workflow step-by-step. We started by reviewing the collection and just brainstorming. We listed out 5-6 options for series organization, and we jotted down any major questions/concerns we had. We then discussed these notes with Allison and Bethany in great length, going through the pros and cons of each option before finalizing on a processing plan.

Throughout this undertaking, we kept in mind that the main intended audience would be student researchers. In particular, we learned early on that Professor Shamin Kwa of the East Asian Languages and Cultures department wanted to teach a course using this collection. Drawing on our own experiences with archival research, we thought that distinguishing materials by scholars, establishing clear chronologies, and creating directories would be helpful. These organizational choices would create entry points into the collection and simplify navigation for researchers. Afterall, making the collection accessible and friendly was vital to the success of the project. 

Ultimately, we decided on an organization that, in our opinion, increased the collection’s cohesion while preserving the materials’ original context. Unfortunately, the Chinese Scholarship Committee Records were inherently porous, and there is overall not a lot of material on Chinese scholars within the College archives. This was frustrating to us as researchers and new archivists, but we did our best to organize a collection that tells the histories of the scholars as holistically as possible. 

We completely reorganized the “General Correspondence” series and retitled it “Series 4: Scholar Files.” With the previous arrangement, it felt like the stories of the Chinese scholars were clumped together, lost within a sea of correspondences. So instead, we assigned each scholar with archival presence their own folder, regardless of the amount of materials available. By doing so, we hope to recognize their individuality in equal regards, to make their lives distinguished, titled, and thus accessible. We also printed a directory of scholars mentioned in every annual report and a timeline of committee officers (compiled by the 2024 interns), which were given their own folder and tucked into “Series 1: Administrative Records.” We did not want the names of the scholars to be confused or forgotten, and this organization would help researchers locate scholars easily for case studies.

One decision we found ourselves questioning was the creation of “Series 3: Admissions.” By the end of our collection evaluations, we both agreed to integrate files from the Admissions Office Records (1QA) of 1893-1941 and 1933-1948 into the collection, because they directly pertained to the development of the Chinese Scholarship. We decided to create “Series 3: Admissions” to accommodate these added files and preserve their original context. This series contains general discussions of matriculation requirements and correspondence with scholar candidates, but it also isolates admissions materials from Liu Fung Kei, Yen Theordora, Bang Vaung-Tsien, Ting Vung Yuin, and two candidates. Out of the four Chinese scholars, only Ting Vung Yuin had materials in “Series 4: Scholar Files,” while the others did not. In the case of Ting, these materials were either letters sent by her decades after Bryn Mawr or by her daughter after her death. We debated extensively over what to do with these admissions files for specific scholars: Should we pull them out into the “Series 4: Scholar Files?” Should we try to extrapolate all admissions materials from Series 4 and create a new sub-series? 

We ultimately decided to leave them within Series 3, alongside the files they were originally housed with. We chose to do this because of culminating factors: Firstly, these specific files contained almost exclusively admissions materials. Notably, they housed the earliest and only entrance exams we have from Chinese scholars, which we felt the need to distinguish. Any related correspondences in these files were often logistical and administrative, revolving around exam arrangements, whereas correspondence from “Series 4: Scholar Files” included more personal exchanges, usually extended by scholars themselves. There is also a difference in time period between the scholar files from Series 3 and Series 4, as the case study on Ting Vung Yuin demonstrates. with the former spanning 1904 to 1934 (early years of the scholarship), and the latter spanning 1941 to 1988 (later years of the scholarship). 

Most importantly, after reviewing Series 3 and Series 4, we thought that these four scholar files play a crucial role in illustrating the early development of the Chinese scholarship, particularly in terms of matriculation standards. During the scholarship’s founding years, the admissions process was tailored to the circumstances of each scholar, with reading lists, examiners, and college prep fluctuating year to year. As a result, these early scholars were directly evoked and discussed by the administration when creating and changing the entrance exams. These broader conversations are also documented in Series 3, and if we took out the scholar exams and put them into Series 4, a lot of context would be lost. If we kept the scholar exams and admissions materials in Series 3, they would still be accessible to researchers using the collection while telling a more cohesive narrative. 

“Series 3: Admissions” also contains a lot of materials from Chinese scholarship candidates who did not come to Bryn Mawr. We wanted to distinguish these candidates from scholars who actually attended Bryn Mawr—Otherwise, things get confusing very quickly. For instance, we have the 1934 entrance exams on Chinese Language and Classics from candidates Chow Ella Yi Chi and Woo Dzoe-ts. These are valuable resources, but we also needed to differentiate them from the other scholar exams. Additionally, Chow Ella Yi Chi was the sister of Chow May, a Chinese scholar herself, and it was sometimes difficult to decipher which sister the committee was discussing, because they were often referred to in conjunction and by surname.

Both of us can agree that intellectual processing was very mentally taxing. For those couple of days, we were constantly making, questioning, and justifying each others’ choices. Especially because it was our first time processing a collection (collaboratively, no less), we were anxious about making the “wrong” decision. In reality, processing can be quite flexible and subjective: While there are overarching criteria, a well-processed collection may look very different depending on the institution, the archivist, and the materials. At the time, we felt a need to solidify the organization right then and there. But even as we were physically processing the collection, we kept discovering new information that we had to adapt our plans around: In fact, once we actually started moving things around, certain choices became more logical, and others not so much.
