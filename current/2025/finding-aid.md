> <center><i>Summarizing the Collection and Our Decisions</i></center>

The final step of reprocessing was creating and publishing a finding aid for the collection, which was very exciting. While the Chinese Scholarship Committee Records already had a finding aid on ArchivesSpace, it was outdated and did not reflect the 2025 reorganization. So we dived headfirst into the records once again, going through the reprocessed collection box by box, inventorying each file onto an Excel spreadsheet for bulk import. The number of files totaled 124 across the entire collection, which was crazy to see.

Next, we began drafting an actual finding aid, which includes extensive notes on scope and contents, biographical/historical context, related materials, and processing decisions. The writing process was lengthy yet rewarding. After immersing ourselves in the collection for one-and-a-half months, we had a pretty intimate understanding of its history and contents. In fact, it was sometimes surprising how naturally the words flowed out onto the page. Bits and pieces of the collection seem to have embedded themselves deep within our brains, and being able to pull all of those disparate thoughts into a single document was extremely gratifying.

At the same time, the finding aid really challenged us to condense and refine our summer’s work. The "Scope and Contents" note encouraged us to revisit each series and weigh their research value, while the "Biographical/Historical" note pushed us to substantiate historical details and clarify the organizational timeline. During our preliminary research, we also did a lot of readings on the power and subjectivity of the archivist in framing historical narratives. With that concept in mind, we put special care into the "Processing Information" note, because we wanted to be very transparent about how and why we have transformed the collection. 

It was tough, but we pulled through: After two days of non-stop typing, we had a nice and compact draft, by which we mean seven pages. It went through Allison and Bethany for edits before getting finalized. We then uploaded the notes and inventory onto ArchivesSpace, making sure to input Subject Headings, Related Names, and other miscellaneous fields. Then, after checking everything over and typing our names under authors (a thrilling moment), we hit “Publish All,” and the finding aid went live!

Since embarking on this summer project, we have become personally invested in the success and completion of the collection. The slow yet steady process of researching, rehousing, and recontextualizing became an act of genuine love and curiosity, a tribute to the community of Asian students who have been historically overlooked at the college. The 2025 “Who Built Bryn Mawr?” project directly followed the opening of the ARCH Project, and as Bryn Mawr continues to navigate dialogues surrounding history and culture, it feels more important than ever for the stories of these Chinese scholars to be a part of that discussion. We hope to include their voices within the narratives we tell about Bryn Mawr, and by doing so, affirm the unique experiences of its Asian and Asian-American students.
