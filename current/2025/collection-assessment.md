> <center><i>Evaluating the Collection and Its Condition</i></center>

---

In order for reprocessing to occur, there first needs to be a collection to reprocess. For us, that was, of course, the Chinese Scholarship Committee Records. It greeted us during the first week of the internship in seven old, largely acidic Hollinger boxes, supplemented by two Bankers boxes of Admissions Office Records, the Liu Fung Kei papers, and some deceased alumnae/i files. We hadn’t know what to expect, and what we found was a surprise for sure.

According to the collection’s former processing note, the Chinese Scholarship Committee records had been roughly processed a decade or so earlier, as part of a training session called “Archives for Non-Archivists” hosted by the Council on Library and Information Resources. It had a digital finding aid on ArchivesSpace, which is the platform that Tri-College uses to catalog their Archives and Manuscripts collections. This finding aid was short and deceptively intuitive at first glance, suggesting a small collection organized down to the file level. However, the actual file divisions were often vague, inconsistent, and unhelpful, with some errors in dates. Folders were either overstuffed or pointlessly specific, while the materials themselves were unordered, often folded, in loose leaf, or with rusty staples. 

Notably, upon opening Box 2, we discovered a mess of index-sized donor cards tied in sticky rubber bands, stuck together with a rolodex and a paper-wrapped committee stamp (all of which had to be rehoused). Box 5, 6, and 7 housed general correspondence from scholars that should be, according to the finding aid, grouped alphabetically by last name. However, this organization was strange in practice: Some files ranged between specific last names, such as “Chang-Chin,” but others ranged between letters, such as “X-Z,” while still others were just labeled “W” or “T.” One file was labeled “N-G” but contained only cards from Polly Ng, while another file was labeled “P-R” but contained just one letter from Ook Kyung Pai, who was neither a scholarship recipient nor Chinese (she was Korean). Most frustratingly, the folders were often packed beyond capacity with stacks of correspondence from different scholars, many of whom had more than enough materials for their own folders.

Allison, the College Archivist and our project supervisor, had also found materials related to the Chinese Scholarship in the Admissions Office Records. These included correspondence between Bryn Mawr and other institutes regarding matriculation standards, entrance exams from Chinese and Japanese students, and letters of interest or recommendation for the scholarship. One of our main objectives this summer would be determining whether or not these files should be added to the pre-existing Chinese Scholarship Committee Records. 

The admissions materials were organized quite sensibly by topic and time, although different issues surfaced. The discussions on scholar admissions had amazing research potential, but they were greatly entwined for both the Chinese and Japanese scholarships. There was a series titled “Requirements for Oriental Students,” and folder labels featured frequent misspellings of the names of Japanese scholars, such as “Karouke” instead of “Kaoruko” or “Jaki” instead of “Taki.” The materials were also older, with some fragile, legal-sized exams that required new folders and new boxes.

These seven boxes and a couple dozen folders would be the heart of our work for the next two months, and as this brief description likely shows, there was sure *a lot* of work to be done. Neither of us had processed a collection before, so questions naturally arose: What exactly did the collection have to offer? Why was it organized this way? How should we improve it? Most importantly, where should we start? Guided by these inquiries and the resources that Special Collections had to offer, we were nervous but in good hands, ready to give the project our best shot.
