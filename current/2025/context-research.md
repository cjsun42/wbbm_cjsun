> <center><i>Immersing Ourselves in the Collection and Its History</i></center>

--- 

In early June and at the start of the internship, we spent a good few weeks just investigating the collection and its historical context. The goal of this preliminary research was to grasp the global currents that shaped Bryn Mawr’s Chinese Scholarship Committee. By learning about the social, cultural, and political factors at play, we would then be able to situate our small piece of college history within the larger trends of world history. Understanding the collection’s historical significance also helped us identify its research value and related subject headings, which can be incredibly useful for researchers. Overarching timelines and motifs compelled us as well, providing inspiration for file-level arrangement.

We began by doing a lot of background readings on the socio-political climate of China at the turn of the 20th century. In particular, we discussed the rise of Chinese nationalism in response to the “Century of Humiliation,” the sponsorship of western exchange as a part of the Self-Strengthening Movement, and the political turmoil that overtook China during and after the collapse of the Qing dynasty. We also focused on the significance of Christian missions in triggering both dialogue and tension between Chinese locals and Western expats. Case studies included many chapters on late Qing from The Search for Modern China by Jonathan D. Spence, a moderately cheesy documentary on the Chinese Exclusion Act, and a very, very cheesy documentary on the Boxer Rebellion.

After establishing this broad historical framework, we zoomed in on its intersection with women’s education specifically, taking a closer look at how cultural ideals and gender roles shifted alongside Chinese interpretations of modernization. We read up on topics such as the New Woman movement in China, the role of mission schools in Chinese women’s education, the timeline of Chinese female students in the United States, and the views of early Chinese feminists and educators. We browsed snippets from Ling long, a fashionable women’s magazine published in Shanghai, and we also screened films that young Chinese women in the early 1900s would have consumed, such as Sports Queen (1934) and New Women (1935). These materials gave us a better sense of how popular media grappled with images of new womanhood. Some of the films had us questioning what we had just watched, nevertheless it was an insightful way to begin the summer.

We also spent some time exploring the lives of individual students, largely through their collection files, their related materials, and some online research. We read through Liu Fung Kei’s lengthy and numerous letters to Mary Pierce, which reflected on her life after Bryn Mawr. They span from the 1920s to the 1950s, and it was really surreal to see her react firsthand to the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Chinese Civil War, and their impact on the common man. We also looked into the experiences of early Chinese American students, such as Marina Yung Kwai (Class of 1930), which presented us with another uniquely nuanced and culturally hybrid perspective. 

The convergent and divergent experiences of these Chinese American students and Chinese scholars highlight the complex societal expectations they each faced, which were greatly influenced by socioeconomic status and cultural background. It was important to us to explore these different perspectives, because each scholar embarked on a unique journey to Bryn Mawr, and their stories supplement one another by comparison. By engaging in this process of research and discovery, we gained an intimate understanding of the generations of Chinese scholars that attended Bryn Mawr, from their victories to their tribulations.
