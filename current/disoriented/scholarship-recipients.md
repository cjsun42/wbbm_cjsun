## Matsuda Michi
1895-1899 (undergraduate), 1908-1910 (graduate)

Born in Kyoto, Matsuda Michi graduated from the First Girls’ High School in Kyoto and Ferris Eigaku School in Yokohama before coming to the United States in 1893. She then attended Miss Stevens’ School in Germantown for two years before entering Bryn Mawr College. Matsuda received the first Scholarship for Japanese women offered by Bryn Mawr. Matsuda’s attempts to share her culture were often derided by the American press. Seattle papers reduced her lecture on Japanese culture to “entertainment,” and the Philadelphia Inquirer described her demonstration of a tea ceremony with Kawai Michi as “Fair Japs Pour Tea.” 

Matsuda graduated with the Class of 1899. After graduation, she taught at Kobe College and Doshisha Girls’ School in Japan. She returned to the USA to complete graduate courses at Bryn Mawr and Columbia University before returning to Doshisha. She taught as a professor at Doshisha for 38 years and retired in 1933. Matsuda spent her last years in her hometown Mineyama, Kyoto and passed away on January 15, 1956 at the age of 89.

## Kawai Michi
1900-1904​ 

Kawai Michi was the daughter of a shinto priest and the granddaughter of the village master of Makkido. Her family converted to Christianity during her childhood, and Kawai’s early education was at mission schools. Prior to her entrance to Bryn Mawr, Kawai spent two years of college preparation at Miss Stevens’ School in Germantown. Upon first arriving at Bryn Mawr, Kawai failed to recognize M. Carey Thomas and bluntly asked who she was; she became known for this incident. Kawai completed her undergraduate degree in 1904. She went on to teach at the Tsuda School for five years before serving as General Secretary of the Young Woman’s Christian Association for Japan from 1912 to 1926. She founded Keisen Junior College in 1929 as a Christian school for girls. Kawai was principal until her death on February 11, 1953.

## Suzuki Utako​
1904-1906​

Suzuki's scholarship was rescinded in her third year after Tsuda and the Philadelphia Committee disagreed about the qualifications to receive the scholarship. The Committee felt Suzuki did not earn her place, and Tsuda wanted more control over who received the scholarship.

---

## Hoshino Ai​
1908-1912​

Hoshino Ai graduated from the Tsuda School before coming to Bryn Mawr. In 1910 and 1911, Hoshino published her writings in Tipyn O’Bob, a Bryn Mawr student literary magazine. Both of her submissions focused on Japanese festivals honoring the dead. She went on to receive her A.B. from Bryn Mawr College in 1912, and she returned to the Tsuda School as a teacher in 1912. Hoshino later continued her education in the USA and obtained her M.A. at Columbia University in 1919. Hoshino was the second president of the Tsuda College from 1928 to 1952. At the same time, she was a leader of the Women’s Suffrage League of Japan for a number of years before becoming chair of the Japan branch of the Pan-Pacific Women’s Association in 1953. She passed away on December 5, 1972 from cardiac insufficiency in Tokyo. 

---

## Hitotsuyanagi Maki ​
1912-1914​

Soon after starting Bryn Mawr, Hitotsuyanagi contracted a severe case of typhoid, which continued to affect her health for the next two years. In 1914, she decided to withdraw from Bryn Mawr. 

---

## Uchida Fumi​
1916-1920​

Uchida almost lost her scholarship status three times. It is possible Uchida was a strong supporter of local women's movements, which the more conservative Committee disapproved of. She was one of the twenty students who were admitted to the English Club and later was offered a scholarship in the English department for graduate studies. She lived in No. 5, Radnor Hall. 

---

## Fujita Taki​
1921-1925​

Fujita Taki attended Miss Kirk’s School for one year before matriculating to Bryn Mawr. In 1922, Fujita wrote an article in the College News asking her fellow students and community to not equate Japanese people with Japan’s political actions: “Some Americans informed me that all Japanese appeared alike to them, but you know we are not. As our appearances are different, our ideas, our opinions and our beliefs as much as yours, Americans, are.” 

Fujita studied History, Economics, and Politics at Bryn Mawr, graduating in 1925. She returned to the college as a special student in 1949 after receiving a fellowship to study administrative problems of women’s colleges in the United States. Fujita was a significant figure in public affairs and Japan’s women’s suffrage movement. She was the Director of the Women’s and Minors’ Bureau of Japan’s Ministry of Labor for three years, starting in 1951. She served many other positions such as the President of the League of Women Voters of Japan, the New Japan Women’s League, and the Research Institute on Women’s Problems. From 1958 to 1959 she was a delegate for Japan’s Mission to the United Nations. She taught at Tsuda College for 25 years and became its fourth president in 1962. Fujita was awarded the First Class Order of the Sacred Treasure in 1984 for her work in women’s suffrage and equal rights. Fujita passed away on January 4, 1993.

## Liu Fung Kei​
1918-1922​

Liu Fung Kei was the first recipient of the Chinese Scholarship. Born in Canton to a well-educated family, Liu attended Canton Christian College prior to her arrival to the USA. Liu, like many of her successors, attended the Shipley School for one year of college preparation prior to matriculation. In 1921, Liu hosted a fundraiser for the Chinese famine, which the College News marketed as a chance to try “strange Chinese food” with a “Chinese stunt” performance afterward. The article was placed directly below a report on the anti-Japanese immigration debate held the previous week by Bryn Mawr students. 

Liu studied Physics and Chemistry at Bryn Mawr, graduating in 1922. She was well-liked and became a paradigm of the scholarship’s potential, setting the precedent for Chinese-American educational exchange at the college. Upon graduation, Liu founded the Yuet Wah Middle School in 1925, hoping to offer children a “true education.” The small school grappled with financial instability, staff shortages, and eventually, the Chinese Civil War, leading to the school’s relocation to Macau. In 1941, Liu left the school after the outbreak of the Pacific War and a major gynecological operation. She continued teaching as an English tutor and was beloved by her pupils, until an unfortunate car accident took her life in 1957.  

---

## Yen Theodora C. ​
1922-1923​

Theodora Yen came from an influential family, with her uncle appointed as Chief Advisor of the president of China in 1923. Yen never attained a degree and left Bryn Mawr to marry her husband. As mandated by the SGA constitution then, students could not be married and attend the college.

---

## Dju Lüh ​
1924-1926​

Dju Lüh was the first graduate student to receive the Chinese Scholarship. Though the scholarship was initially intended for undergraduate studies, the committee received many recommendations for graduate scholars as well, with Dju being one of them. Graduating from Ginling College in 1922, she was the niece of His Excellency Hsiung Hsi-Ling and highly praised by the Committee on Foreign Study for Chinese Women. Unfortunately, Dju struggled to adapt in terms of language and academics: She spent her second year of the scholarship completing advanced undergraduate work instead, failing to obtain her M.A. The Chinese Scholarship Committee subsequently refused to sponsor graduate scholars until 1937.

## Marina Yung Kwai
1926-1930

Marina Yung Kwai was the first Asian American student to attend Bryn Mawr. She came from a well-educated diplomatic family: Her father, Yung Kwai, was a longtime diplomat of the Chinese legation and came from a Confucian scholar family. As a participant of the Qing dynasty’s Chinese Educational Mission, he was also one of the earliest Chinese students in the United States. He was later dismissed from the mission and disowned by his father for publicly practicing Christianity. The marriage of Marina Yung Kwai’s parents, Yung Kwai and Mary Burnham, also stirred up controversy in the society papers as one of the few interracial marriages at the time. 

During her time at the College, Marina Yung Kwai was a member of the lacrosse and archery team. She ranked third in her class and graduated Magna cum Laude with an A.B. in French, going on to receive her masters in Romance Languages from Radcliffe College in 1931. Marina Yung Kwai married Wilbur Carl Sze, the first Chinese American Marine Corps officer, and settled in the Washington D.C. area where she grew up. She passed away on December 29, 2001.
